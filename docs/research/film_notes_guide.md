# A guide to writing film notes

Draft help copy for Glutton. The last section describes planned aggregate analysis and is future-facing until that capability ships.

## Start with your reaction

Your rating tells Glutton how much you enjoyed a film. A note gives Glutton a chance to understand why, so it can look for more of what you enjoy and learn what to steer away from. A sentence about one thing you remember is enough. You can rate now and come back to add or change your notes later.

> What stayed with you? If you can put your finger on it, what made you feel that way?

Use these prompts whenever they help you find the words.

## Say what you felt and what brought it on

“Creepy” is a good start. If you remember what made it creepy, include the detail. It might be a sound, a performance, the camera keeping something out of sight, the pace of a conversation, the look of a place, or a decision that made you feel complicit.

- “That low hum kept me waiting for something to go wrong, even in ordinary scenes.”
- “The bright courtyard made me feel more exposed. I kept looking for somewhere they could hide.”
- “I felt strangely at home in the spooky hotel. The warm lamps and long stretches of quiet made me want to stay.”

## Say whether you wanted more of it

The same feeling can be the reason you loved a film or the reason you switched it off. Let Glutton know which it was. Mixed reactions are welcome.

- “I loved the slow build while everyone at dinner seemed to be hiding something. Once I knew who to believe, I lost interest.”
- “The gore made us laugh and worked perfectly for movie night. The same scenes would have put me off if the film had played them straight.”
- “I wanted the sadness to turn into relief. The film left me feeling hollow, and I’d rather avoid that tonight.”

## Notice when the feeling changed

An opening can be playful, a middle stretch can feel oppressive, and an ending can change how you remember the whole film. If a feeling arrived late, kept escalating, or followed you afterwards, say when.

- “I was calm while I watched it. Later that night every half-open door in my flat looked wrong.”
- “The ending let me breathe again after an hour of waiting for something terrible. That release was what I loved.”

If the ending matters, you can describe the effect without revealing the twist. In your notes, include whatever you want to remember.

## Compare experiences and mention the occasion

A comparison can explain what you mean by a word like “cozy”, “slow”, or “intense”. The occasion can matter too. A film you turn on every October, a film your friends want to talk through, and a film you want to watch alone with the lights off can all work for different reasons.

- “I go back to this one because I know all the scares. The first time through it was anything but cozy.”
- “I usually like one-room horror, but I need to understand where the exits are. Here I could never get my bearings.”
- “I’d like more films where a group keeps guessing together. Everyone had a different theory, and we were still arguing about it afterwards.”

## Use your own words

Write down the part that’s hard to describe. “It felt like being wide awake in someone else’s house after everyone had gone to bed” gives Glutton more to work with than a label you picked because it was close enough. Include what you missed, what surprised you, what you wanted less of, or something you wish more films would do. Your reaction can change on a rewatch. Both reactions are worth remembering.

## How notes help your recommendations today

Glutton uses the notes you save to build your personal taste profile and interpret your ratings. A note can help Glutton understand that you liked the sense of distrust in a film, for example, and want that feeling again even in a different kind of story. Your notes are optional and aren’t published or shared with other users. You can add or revise them after rating a film. ## How your notes will help improve Glutton

Once this feature is available, Glutton will look for recurring patterns across viewers’ notes. If several viewers describe the same feeling or point to the same detail, that pattern can help us describe what it feels like to watch the film. For example, different viewers might notice that the lighting and quiet rooms make a haunted house feel inviting. If that distinction comes up across films and helps people find something they want to watch, it could become a new quality.

Be specific when you can, and write what you actually felt. If everyone calls a film cozy and you found it oppressive, say what gave you that feeling. It helps us understand where reactions differ and what viewers are responding to.

Your notes aren’t published or shared with other users. Glutton will use recurring patterns to improve shared film descriptions and qualities. Glutton’s standard terms and Privacy Notice will explain this use.

### Product direction before launch

Aggregate learning from notes is planned for early implementation. Writing notes remains optional. Analysis for shared film enrichment is a standard use of notes under the product terms, with disclosure in the Privacy Notice. Shared film evidence should describe a recurring pattern supported by distinct viewers, with no publication of individual notes, quotations or identities. Preserve material differences in reactions. Keep personal taste interpretation in Ratings and shared film evidence and accepted qualities in Registry. Registry can use supported aggregate descriptions as additional evidence when JEV reassesses a film. Patterns that recur across films and would change what viewers want to watch can inform new quality definitions and exclusions.

The pipeline is still to be designed. Decide what constitutes sufficient independent evidence, how revisions and deletions affect support and retained aggregates, when evidence becomes eligible for reassessment, and how to validate assignments before publication. Align the actual terms, Privacy Notice and help copy with the implemented behaviour before enabling the feature.
